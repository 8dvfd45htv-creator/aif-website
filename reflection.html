What I learnt

What I know now that I did not at the start, what I learnt about myself, and what I still cannot say.

What I know now

More than I expected, and a lot of it came from my own survey before I read anything.

  • Motivation is not stable. All 16 people who answered Question 13 said theirs changes with what they are doing.
  • People describe their motivation as coming from themselves, but when asked about specific experiences, 17 of 19 said pressure from a parent, teacher or coach had pushed them harder than they would push themselves.
  • Motivation regularly turns on people. 15 of the 16 people who answered Question 12, and one person in Question 11, described something that motivated them until it made things worse.
  • My peers rated the harm of pressure higher than its usefulness, 4.58 against 3.42. I had assumed pressure was effective but risky. They see it as unreliable and risky.
  • The research gives me reasons. The kind of motivation matters more than the amount. A reward that you expect stops producing a signal, and wanting can outlive liking. And whether you endorse your own motivation is a different question from whether anyone is forcing you.

What I learnt about myself

Swimming was not laziness. For six years I thought I was someone who gave up. I now understand it was the kind of motivation I had. All of my reasons were external: my parents' encouragement, the money, not wanting to let anyone down. A published study of competitive swimmers describes what happened to me before I understood it. I was not lazy. I just never wanted it.

I should ask whose reason it is. Frankfurt gave me a question I can use: do I want to want this? Applied to my life, it is the difference between swimming and the gym. It is a question I can ask about future choices, including what to study and do after school.

Other people change how hard I try, and I did not notice. My diary showed me I train harder with friends than alone, doing the identical workout. I used to think motivation was something I had or did not have. It is also something my environment does to me.

I make excuses that sound reasonable. The leg day entry showed me that low motivation does not always feel like low motivation. It feels like a sensible plan.

I talked myself out of the hard bits of my own project. I did not chase my emails. I did not write my diary. I polished things that were already finished while my reading sat at zero. The parts with nobody waiting on them are the parts that stopped. I read about relatedness and outside structure, and then showed it in my own planning without noticing.

Face to face took more nerve than email, and it paid off. I wanted to stay behind a screen because it felt safer. Talking to my philosophy teacher properly built confidence I did not have going in, and I got an answer I would not have got in writing.

I am less lost than I was, and I think I understand why. I do not think the answer to feeling lost is finding one fixed meaning. My dad said that everything being pointless is not a reason to give up, it is why you choose what you care about. Autonomy is the word psychology uses for that. I wrote about it here.

Every part of this pointed at the same thing. My diary, my survey, my reading and my own timeline all say motivation depends on whether the reason is yours.

What I still do not know

  • My survey measured opinions about motivation more than motivation itself, and I have no way to check whether the answers are consistent.
  • I do not know if my findings hold outside Year 11 boys at one school who know me.
  • I read summaries of the philosophers, not the full original texts, and the brain section borrows mechanisms from animal studies. The stress and prefrontal cortex idea is a plausible mechanism, not a finding.
  • I do not know how much of the pressure my peers described comes from other people and how much from themselves. A teacher suggested it may be a lot more internal than I assumed.
  • I still cannot say what makes someone stop. Atomic Habits explains how habits are built and does not explain that, and neither does my survey.

What I will take with me

  • Research skills. Writing a survey, then changing it when it was not answering the right question. Reading a source that disagrees with me on purpose. Saying plainly what a source cannot show.
  • Contacting experts. Asking questions a person can actually answer, and starting early enough that one delay does not wreck the plan.
  • Interviewing in person. Listening for the moment someone changes their answer.
  • Time management. Giving every open ended task a date, and using a plan as a record I update instead of a forecast I forget.
  • The question about whose reason it is. I think it is the most useful thing I take from the project, and it applies to study choices, sport, and what I do after school.
