Conversations

Three teachers, a careers counsellor, and my peers, and what each one changed in my thinking.

  • Psychology teacher

  • A third teacher
  • Teacher, counsellor, peers
  • How they fit together

I wanted two kinds of expert for the two halves of my learning goal. Psychology explains how motivation works. Philosophy covers the question psychology cannot answer, which is whether someone acting under pressure is really choosing. A third teacher added a view from watching students for years. I have named people by role on this site.

My psychology teacher: how motivation is measured

I emailed five questions on 12 August. She replied that most of them went beyond her background and would be better answered by journal articles or specialists, and she answered only the last one, on how motivation is measured.

Motivation can be measured in surveys either by using Likert scales (scale of 1 to 10) or open ended questions (collect qualitative data (worded) that is also subjective)... both are subject to social desirability bias (so that people may answer how they think they should).

My psychology teacher, by email (shortened)

What I took from it. At first it felt like a letdown. But a psychology teacher saying most of my questions went beyond her shows how specialised the area is, and it backed up a suspicion I had from my own survey: self-report data has real limits. Her answer on measurement was the most useful single thing I was given. It explained the gap in my own results between multiple choice answers, where nobody picked pressure or proving someone wrong, and the anonymous written answers, where several people admitted exactly that. I had noticed the pattern and did not know why it happens.

She also named three real motivation surveys I had never heard of. That gave me something to compare my survey against.

Because of her reply I rewrote my questions for my philosophy teacher to ask what philosophers argue, rather than what the evidence shows.

My philosophy teacher: whether pressure is a real choice

I emailed him on 12 August and followed up on 31 August, which was later than I should have. He offered to meet in person, and the interview was on Friday 4 September. I had planned to stay behind a screen because it felt safer. Going face to face was the right call.

Is someone afraid of the consequences really choosing?

I suppose it depends on whether they're having a pre-existing motivation strengthened, or whether they're having a new motivation created. That's the real question.

My philosophy teacher

Halfway through his answer he started to say the only way you can really be forced is physically, then corrected himself to say what we really mean is coercion. An email would only have given me his finished answer. Being in the room let me watch him work it out. His answer also splits my headline number. The 17 out of 19 who said pressure pushed them harder than they would push themselves is not one result. For some people it strengthens a drive that was already there. For others it creates a motive that was not there until the pressure arrived.

Does it matter why someone does something?

I think it matters why someone does something, because it influences future outcomes... If someone buys bread because they're out of the pasta, the thing they really wanted, then that's different from someone who buys bread because they love bread.

My philosophy teacher

Do you need struggle for life to feel meaningful?

I'd say as a rule, it's positive experiences after suffering that make the suffering interesting. Like, if you're still suffering, you're probably not finding much meaning. But if you're able to get over that suffering and recover, then when you look back... you probably do have meaning.

My philosophy teacher

This was the best thing he said, and it was not in my project before. It is whether you recover, not struggle itself, that makes meaning. Swimming was struggle with no recovery, years of getting worse until I quit. The gym is struggle I keep choosing and keep recovering from. He also said there is definitely a difference between struggle you picked and struggle that happened to you. (Nussbaum disagrees in an interesting way.)

Why do people give up things they were properly into?

Rousseau talks about amour-propre, which is this self-love that's built on comparison... if being good at something is part of how you see yourself, then hitting a wall doesn't just mean you're worse at swimming, it threatens that whole self-image. And that's often easier to just walk away from than sit with.

My philosophy teacher

This surprised me most. It answered something I had wondered since Week 1. Quitting swimming was never only about getting worse at swimming. Getting worse threatened how I saw myself, and walking away was easier than sitting inside it.

Where he sent me

He gave me two philosophers: Martha Nussbaum for suffering and meaning, and Harry Frankfurt for whether being pressured still counts as choosing, "whether you endorse your own motivation or you're just being carried along by it. That's basically the swimming versus the gym thing you opened with." I read both: see the philosophy section.

Limit. This was one conversation, recorded informally on a phone. It is a perspective and a starting point for further reading, not evidence on its own. The written transcript was tidied with an AI tool, as I explain on the credits page.

A third teacher: what she sees in students

I emailed her four questions and she answered all of them in writing.

  • What keeps students going. A reason that matters to them, enjoying the subject or seeing how it connects to their future. She also named self-efficacy: believing you can succeed, with enough challenge to stay interesting but not so much that success feels impossible. Students with sport, work, friends and other interests often manage well.
  • Pressure. She asked what my 17 students meant by pressure. Encouragement, expectations and accountability can push you to do more. Excessive pressure increases stress. Fear of disappointing someone, or feeling your worth depends on results, is quite different. She also said that in her experience a lot of pressure comes from students themselves, especially high achievers.
  • Marks to caring. The emphasis on the ATAR can make students strategic ("what do I need to do to get an A?"). The shift to caring happens when they find something they enjoy or get curious. But students are so time-poor they rarely get to learn something just because it interests them.
  • Unmotivated or burnt out. A burnt-out student may have started as one of the most motivated, pushed too hard for too long, and low motivation is a symptom of burnout rather than where it started. It is important to ask why a student is not motivated rather than labelling them.

Find your own reason for doing what you're doing, and keep some balance in your life.

My third teacher, her advice to a Year 11

What I took from it. She did not accept my best-known number without asking what it meant. My psychology teacher had questioned how precise my survey was, so two teachers independently found the same limitation: Question 5 was closed choice and I never defined pressure. I did ask two open questions elsewhere. Question 11 had answers about fear of failing and not wanting to disappoint people, and Question 12 had answers about being pushed until it became fear of letting someone down. So I have some of what she wanted, but it sits in separate questions.

She also reversed an assumption of mine. I had treated pressure as something that comes from outside the student. If a lot of it comes from within, then the fear of failing in my own Question 11 answers may never have been external. And her split between burnout and low motivation changed how I read my swimming. Some of the flatness near the end may not have been low motivation at all but burnout from years of pressure and paid results, only becoming visible late.

Her advice lines up with the autonomy idea in self-determination theory. Limit: she is one teacher, shaped by the students she has taught. I cannot generalise from it.

My class teacher, careers counsellor and peers

My class teacher

In Week 1 she introduced intrinsic and extrinsic motivation, which I had not thought about. That turned out to be the split self-determination theory is built on, so a conversation at the start sits under my whole research section. She also suggested a video, keeping a diary, and trying strategies on myself, and she pushed me to think about my audience.

My careers counsellor

She suggested a website structured like a thesis. Her reasoning was that building something across a semester gives the project a clear goal, which is itself a form of motivation. A website can hold the research, survey, interviews and diary in one place.

Peers

I had six peer conversations. Four were early, about my topic.

  • Being asked whether I meant sport, school or everyday life showed I had never decided. I chose everyday life, because you can walk away from sport and school but not from everyday life.
  • Being asked how the project would help others made me realise I had been treating it as only something I wanted to learn. That pushed me to make something other people could use.
  • Being asked why I chose motivation made me say out loud that I had struggled with it myself.
  • Being asked how I would measure motivation, and whether people would be honest, led directly to making the survey anonymous and sent first to close friends. I still did not have a proper answer on measurement, which is why I went to my psychology teacher.

Two later conversations were about my output. Being asked who the website is for narrowed it to Year 11 and 12, because that is when the pressure builds, and made me connect the audience to my survey finding. Being asked how I would avoid sounding like I recommend pressure and bullying made me decide the site has to say plainly that these methods sometimes work for some people and damage others. I also had to admit I had not built a website before, and that my academic reading had not started yet. A peer also asked what happens if the research says something different to my data. I said that would be more interesting than if it agreed.

How they fit together

My psychology teacher gave me method: how motivation is measured and why my own results look the way they do. My philosophy teacher gave me the question psychology cannot answer: whether pressured motivation is a real choice. My third teacher gave me the view from the classroom, and the burnout point nobody else raised. My peers were both my evidence and the people who challenged me most. Everyone else mostly confirmed what I was already doing.
