Self-determination theory: the kind of motivation matters

I swam for years. My parents encouraged me and I got rewards for doing well, and for a while that was enough to get me to training. But none of the reasons were really mine. I slowly got worse and in the end I quit. I always thought I just did not have enough motivation. Now I think the problem was the kind of motivation I had, not how much.

I used to think of motivation like fuel. Some people have lots, some have a little, and you fix it by adding pressure or a reward. Self-determination theory (SDT), by Edward Deci and Richard Ryan, says that is the wrong way to look at it. There are different types of motivation, and the type matters more than the amount (Ryan & Deci, 2000).

Say two students study for three hours for the same exam. One finds the subject interesting and the other only wants the ATAR. They look the same from the outside, but SDT says they will be different in how long they keep going, how well they understand the work and how they feel while doing it.

The three needs

SDT says everyone has three basic needs, in the same way we need food and sleep.

Autonomy is feeling like your choices are yours. It does not mean doing everything alone. You can choose to take advice or follow a rule you agree with. It just has to feel like your own decision. One survey answer said this well: motivation does not just turn up because something is important, you have to care about it yourself.

Competence is feeling like you are good at something or getting better at it. Tasks that are a bit hard help. Tasks that are impossible or boring do not. One person in my survey said that if they understand something and can feel themselves improving, they keep going, but if they keep getting things wrong they lose motivation fast. It also explains swimming against the gym. In swimming I got worse. In the gym I can see progress every week.

Relatedness is feeling connected to other people. I did not expect this one to matter so much. In diary entry 1 I was not going to train until my friends messaged me, and then I had one of my best sessions. Entry 2 was the same workout alone and it felt completely different. A lot of people in my survey said friends are why they keep showing up.

If a place supports all three needs, SDT says you get better motivation and wellbeing. If it blocks them, people switch off. Swimming had almost none of the three for me. The gym has all of them.

Types of motivation

Most people split motivation into intrinsic (you enjoy it) and extrinsic (you do it for a reward). SDT says extrinsic has levels:

  • External: "I'll get in trouble if I don't."
  • Introjected: "I'd feel guilty and useless if I skipped it."
  • Identified: "This actually matters for what I want to do."
  • Integrated: "This is just who I am."
  • Intrinsic: "I like doing it."

The big split is autonomous motivation (identified, integrated, intrinsic) against controlled motivation (external, introjected). Autonomous motivation helps people stick with things. Controlled motivation leads to doing the minimum and then quitting (Ryan & Deci, 2000).

Introjected is the tricky one because it feels like it comes from you. The guilt and pressure are in your own head, so it seems like your own motivation. SDT still counts it as controlled, because you are being pushed, not choosing.

This explained the gap in my survey. Nobody said they push through things to prove someone wrong, but 17 said it had motivated them when I asked directly. People described their motivation the way it feels from the inside, and introjected motivation feels like it is yours. So the two sets of answers do not actually clash.

Most useful things are not fun all the time, so the aim is not to enjoy everything. It is to move your reasons along that list, from being forced toward agreeing with why you are doing it.

The evidence

Rewards can backfire. Deci (1971) gave university students a puzzle they already found interesting. One group was paid for solving it and the other was not. Later, in a break when nobody seemed to be watching, the paid group spent less time on the puzzle. Getting paid had turned something they liked into work. Praise did not cause the same drop, so the problem is not encouragement, it is being controlled by a reward. That is close to what happened to me. I was rewarded for doing well and the reward slowly became the reason I was there.

Sport shows the same thing. Pelletier and others (2001) followed competitive swimmers for 21 months. The swimmers with more autonomous motivation were more likely to keep going, and the ones with more controlled motivation were more likely to drop out.

I found my own version of this in my survey. One peer wrote that their coach pushed them so hard that they ended up quitting their sport. And nearly all of the Question 12 answers (see the survey) describe motivation moving from autonomous toward controlled.

Problems with the theory

Cameron and Pierce (1994) looked at the same research and said the reward effect was much smaller than Deci and Ryan claimed. Most people now sit in the middle. Rewards hurt motivation mainly when the task was already interesting and the reward was promised ahead of time. Paying someone to do a boring job nobody would do for free is not really a problem.

A lot of SDT research also relies on people reporting their own reasons, and that is a fair criticism. My survey backs it up, because my multiple choice and written questions gave almost opposite answers from the same 19 people.

SDT also does not explain something for me. My peers were much more sure that pressure is harmful (4.58) than that it is effective (3.42). That is why I went to the brain research next.

What this changes for the site. The better question is not "how do I get more motivated?" It is what kind of motivation you have, and whether your situation is supporting it or wearing it down.

How motivation is actually measured

I did not think of this myself. I asked my psychology teacher how motivation is measured, and her reply named three tools I had never heard of: the Intrinsic Motivation Inventory (IMI), the Work Extrinsic and Intrinsic Motivation Scale (WEIMS), and the Motivated Strategies for Learning Questionnaire (MSLQ). I would not have found them on my own because I did not know they existed.

I looked mostly at the IMI because it is closest to what I was doing (McAuley et al., 1989). The MSLQ is about motivation for school, which is most of what my year level deals with (Pintrich et al., 1991). The WEIMS is made for workplaces, so it was the least useful (Tremblay et al., 2009).

Reading them showed me what my survey does not do. They ask several questions about the same idea, so one strange answer does not decide the result. They include reverse-worded questions to catch people who agree with everything. They keep the same scale the whole way through, while mine switched between 1 to 10 and 1 to 5. And they combine questions into one score for each idea, where I used single questions.

Mine did some things well. It was anonymous, and it mixed multiple choice with written answers, which is what picked up the gap between Questions 3 and 6. Questions 12 and 13 asked what happens after motivation changes, which none of those tools ask.

My honest conclusion is that my survey measured opinions about motivation more than motivation itself. My teacher also told me that averages get pulled around by extreme answers and medians do not, so I now show both. If I ran it again I would ask a few questions for each idea and keep one scale.

Dopamine and the brain

SDT told me what kind of motivation I had in swimming and why being paid for results was a problem. It did not tell me why the rewards stopped working. I wanted to see if anything sat underneath it. This section is shorter than the SDT one because I am borrowing one idea, not learning a whole theory.

Reward prediction error

Wolfram Schultz spent years recording single dopamine neurons in monkeys. He found they do not really respond to the reward itself. They respond to the difference between the reward you got and the reward you expected. They fire when a reward is better than expected, stay at normal when it is exactly as expected, and drop when it is worse (Schultz, 2016). The part that matters to me is the normal level. The reward is still there, but the signal is not (BrainFacts, 2021).

That is swimming. I got rewarded when I did well. At first it was a surprise, so it worked. After a while it was just what happened when I swam well, so I expected it and it did nothing. The reward kept coming and stopped meaning anything. By then it had also become my reason for being there, which is the SDT side of the problem. The two ideas fit together. SDT explains why the reason was weak. Reward prediction error explains why it wore out.

It also means something uncomfortable. For a reward to keep working it has to keep beating what you already expect, and nobody is going to keep making your reward bigger forever.

Wanting is not liking

Kent Berridge and Terry Robinson argue that the brain circuits for wanting a reward are separate from the ones for liking it, and that dopamine drives the wanting, not the pleasure. Wanting runs on big, strong systems that include dopamine. Liking runs on much smaller and more fragile systems that do not need dopamine (Berridge & Robinson, 2016).

Saying "dopamine is not the pleasure chemical" does not help much by itself. What is useful is that wanting and liking can come apart, so you can want something you have stopped enjoying. That fits Question 12, where people wrote about good results turning into pressure to keep a standard, being pushed until it became fear of letting someone down, and competition until they could not be happy with their own progress. Each one is someone still chasing something they have stopped liking.

Stress and the prefrontal cortex

This is my attempt at explaining the pressure gap, and I am less sure about it. Arnsten (2009) looked at evidence that even fairly mild stress you cannot control quickly shuts down the prefrontal cortex, which is the part of the brain used for planning. The same stress chemicals make the emotional part (the amygdala) and the habit part (the striatum) stronger. So behaviour moves from thought through to automatic. The word that stood out to me was "uncontrollable". That is autonomy again, just described by a neuroscientist.

If pressure puts someone in that state, it would explain both halves of my numbers. You still get behaviour, because habits and threat responses still make people do things, so pressure does not look like it does nothing. What you lose is planning and flexibility, which is the part that makes studying work. Students feel the harm strongly because they feel the state, but rate the effectiveness lower because what it produced was not good work.

I am reaching here. Arnsten is about working memory under stress in animals and lab tasks, not Year 11 students being nagged about their ATAR. It is a possible reason, not a finding about my survey.

Problems

  • Levels of explanation. Saying "dopamine did it" is just describing the same event in different words. Brain explanations sound more final than psychology ones, and they should not. SDT still does most of the work. This section only adds an idea underneath it.
  • Animals. Almost all the reward prediction error and wanting and liking research is on monkeys and rats. Being paid for a swim time is not a drop of juice. There is human brain imaging that supports reward prediction error (Schultz, 2016), but I cannot say I have closed the gap.
  • Pop psychology. Dopamine detoxes, phones destroying your reward system, "dopamine hits": nearly all of it is made up or a twisted version of the papers above. I decided early not to use any of it, and finding the real sources under a topic the internet has messed up was most of the work.

A reward has to keep getting bigger to keep working. And wanting something is not the same as liking it.

Two philosophers my teacher pointed me to

My philosophy teacher recommended both of these, one for each of the questions I needed the most help with. Frankfurt is about choice against compulsion. Nussbaum is about struggle and meaning. You can read the interview they came from.

Harry Frankfurt: wanting something and wanting to want it

Frankfurt separates first-order desires (wanting something) from second-order desires (wanting to want it). Someone can want a cigarette and also want not to want it. For Frankfurt, freedom is not about whether anyone is forcing you. It is about whether what you want matches what you would agree with if you stepped back and thought about it. If it does, he calls it acting wholeheartedly. If it does not, you are divided against yourself even though nobody is making you do anything.

That matches swimming against the gym almost exactly. In swimming I wanted to keep training, because of the rewards and not wanting to let my parents down. But if I had asked myself whether I wanted to want that, the honest answer was no. That is being divided, even though nothing was forcing me. The gym is the opposite. I want to go, and when I think about it I am happy that I want to.

It also changes how I read my survey. The 17 out of 19 who said pressure pushed them harder than they would push themselves could be two groups under one yes: students who are wholehearted about the push, and students who are divided, doing it while wishing they did not want to.

Martha Nussbaum: emotions, vulnerability and meaning

Nussbaum sees emotions as judgements about what matters to you, not just blind feelings. Fear, grief and love all include a kind of thinking, because they show that something you care about is at stake. So she argues that being vulnerable is not a flaw to get rid of in a good life. It is the cost of caring about anything outside yourself. A life built to avoid all vulnerability would have nothing in it you loved enough to lose.

This adds to what my teacher said, which was that struggle only gives meaning once you have recovered from it. For Nussbaum the struggle itself, while you are still in it, can already be meaningful, because it shows you correctly valued something real. Grief that is not over is not meaningless. It is accurately tracking a real loss.

It adds something to swimming against the gym, and to the teacher's point about burnout in the third interview. The exhaustion from pushing for years might have been tracking something true, which is that the activity mattered enough to be worth the cost.

Limit. I read summaries and excerpts of both philosophers, not their full original books. Given the time I had, that is a real limit.

Atomic Habits, the source that disagrees with me

I bought Atomic Habits by James Clear on 27 August. Clear says himself it is a manual and not academic research. That told me how to use it: for practical ideas, not as evidence.

The first idea I marked was his four-step model of habits: cue, craving, response, reward. In my words, something triggers you, you want the outcome, you act, and you get something back. It fits my diary. In entries 1 and 2 my friends messaging me was the cue, and when the cue was gone, how hard I trained changed. Entry 3 is a loop that never started, because leg day is something I do not enjoy.

The other two ideas go together. Clear argues that small changes add up to remarkable results if you stick with them for years. His graph puts numbers on it: getting 1% better every day for a year is 1.01 to the power of 365, which is about 37.78, against 1% worse every day, which is about 0.03. He says consistency beats motivation. This is the first source I read that disagrees with my project. He would say asking what drives people is the wrong question, because if your system is right you do not need drive.

But both ideas depend on you keeping going for years. My swimming was consistent for years and still collapsed, and 16 of my 19 respondents said pressure had made them want to quit entirely, at least sometimes (Question 7). Clear explains how habits are built. He does not explain what makes someone stop.

Happiness, meaning and feeling lost

When I wrote about feeling lost, I went looking for proof that happiness was the answer. What I found was more useful.

  • Hedonic adaptation. Brickman and Campbell (1971) described how people go back to about the same level of happiness after almost anything, good or bad. Brickman, Coates and Janoff-Bulman (1978) found that lottery winners and people who had just become paralysed both drifted back toward where they started. Getting what you want changes less than you expect, for less time than you expect.
  • Meaning over happiness. Viktor Frankl (1959) was a psychiatrist who survived the Holocaust. He drew a hard line between happiness and meaning. He thought chasing happiness directly does not work, and that people can get through almost anything if they have a reason to.
  • Relationships. Harvard's Study of Adult Development has followed the same people since 1938. Its director, Robert Waldinger (2015), says the clearest finding is that close relationships predict a good life better than money, success or fame.
  • Gratitude. Emmons and McCullough (2003) had people write a few things they were grateful for each week. After ten weeks they reported higher wellbeing than people who wrote about daily annoyances, even though nothing in their lives had changed.
  • Self-compassion. Neff (2003) found that people who treated themselves with the patience they would give a friend recovered faster from setbacks. It was not because they cared less about doing well, but because they were not also fighting themselves.

Camus (1942) argues in The Myth of Sisyphus that having no built-in meaning is not a reason to give up. It is what makes choosing mean anything. My dad said almost the same thing to me, which you can read about in the piece on feeling lost.
